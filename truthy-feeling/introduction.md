# That Truthy Feeling: Toward a Physicalist Ontology

*Start here · A complete introduction · No specialist background required*

<div class="reading-note">By Fred Smalkin, Jr. · Working essay developed with substantial AI assistance. CST is a proposal; the cited studies do not establish a distinct correspondence schema.</div>

## Before you read

This introduction stands alone. It explains the proposed account without equations, including the difference between partial satisfaction and uncertainty, and why an assessment might guide what we investigate next.

For readers familiar with LessWrong, two useful connections are [*The Useful Idea of Truth*](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/XqvnWFtRD2keJdwjX/the-useful-idea-of-truth) and [*How An Algorithm Feels From Inside*](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/yA4gF5KrboK2m2Xu7/how-an-algorithm-feels-from-inside). Neither is required preparation.

---

You look at a glass of water and see that there is room for more. You pour, stopping before it reaches the rim. Your understanding of the glass has guided a successful action.

Start with that ordinary competence. We can recognize objects, understand descriptions, and use what we know to answer questions. We need not reproduce every detail of the world in our heads. We need to preserve the distinctions that matter to what we are doing.

How does a physical brain accomplish this? And what happens when its assessment becomes available to us as the sense that something is right?

**Correspondence Schema Theory, or CST, proposes that a mind maintains a simplified account of how a claim stands: what supports it, where it applies, and what remains uncertain. That account helps guide both judgment and further inquiry. Where satisfaction is genuinely graded, it may preserve a degree rather than only a yes or no.**

The starting point is successful evaluation. Its failures, and the forces that can pull an assessment away from its grounds, come later.

## Partly full is not partly sure

Imagine that the glass holds 250 milliliters and contains exactly 200. You know how much water is there: it is 80 percent filled.

That is different from being 80 percent sure that a hidden glass is filled to the brim. In one case, the condition has a known degree. In the other, you are uncertain whether an all-or-nothing condition holds.

Fuzzy logic provides ways of working with degrees of applicability. For this example, we can agree that “fullness” means the fraction of the glass occupied by water. A better measurement could make our estimate more precise, but it would not make the glass any fuller. Developed accounts of graded semantics investigate how such degrees relate to the meanings of claims, rather than treating every intermediate value as uncertainty.[1]

Ordinary language introduces another question. Someone saying “the glass is full” may mean that there is no room left. We must establish what the claim says before evaluating it. The measured amount does not dictate how every use of the word *full* works.

CST takes seriously the possibility that some interpreted claims fit their subject to a degree, even when the relevant facts are known. That is its preferred hypothesis—not something the glass example establishes by itself.

## A detective who never lived

Consider another familiar judgment: “Sherlock Holmes is a detective.”

That sounds right, although Holmes was never a living person. His nonexistence is not a small factual qualification to the life of an otherwise ordinary detective. It raises a genuine question: what makes a description of him correct?

The stories supply grounds for calling him a detective. They also constrain what else we can say. Some descriptions are supported, others contradicted, and some questions left open.[2]

We often leave “in these stories” unsaid because the conversation already supplies it. Making that qualification explicit helps identify the claim; it does not require another physical universe containing Holmes.

Other claims depend on other conditions: a place, time, rule, or intended meaning. Understanding those conditions is part of understanding what is being assessed. Clarification can recover a question we already intended to ask, or help us agree on a different one. It does not necessarily provide new evidence about the original question.

## The map, the territory, and the person reading it

A forecast is not the weather. Our confidence in the forecast is not a property of the weather either.

This is the familiar map–territory distinction: a representation differs from what it represents. But the person consulting the forecast is also part of the physical world. Their judgment that it is relevant and trustworthy is another process to explain.

LessWrong’s existing treatments already develop this physical perspective on beliefs, including beliefs about our own beliefs and the processes that produce them. CST builds on that work rather than replacing a mere metaphor with the first scientific account.[3]

The question pursued here is what an assessment preserves. Does it retain only “probably dry,” or also the place, period, source, and limits that make the forecast useful?

That information matters when the next question arrives.

## A mind needs more than an answer

Suppose you are planning a walk. A forecast for your area gives you a reasonable basis for deciding whether to take a coat. Planning a longer trip may call for a wider forecast and more checking.

The immediate answer and the decision to investigate further are connected. Your assessment can include both what the forecast predicts and how far it supports the decision you need to make.

CST calls the proposed internal model of that assessment a *correspondence schema*. It might preserve something like “this is well supported for the next hour,” rather than only “yes.” It need not preserve those words as an internal sentence; they describe the information the account proposes a system can use.

This has substantial predecessors. Mangan connected felt rightness with contextual fit, retrieval, and the regulation of thought. Nelson and Narens developed a framework in which monitoring informs a model used to control cognition. Attention Schema Theory supplies a related proposal about a simplified model participating in the control of attention.[4]

CST brings those resources into a more explicit account of evaluation. Incoming information constrains the judgment; knowledge and purposes guide what is considered; and represented information about the assessment helps determine what to do next. There is no little observer inside the head inspecting the whole process.

## Degrees and categorical answers

Return to a glass that is nearly full. Remove one drop. It still seems nearly full. Keep going, however, and eventually it is empty.

This is a version of Sorites, the paradox of the heap. A graded account distinguishes “almost unchanged” from “unchanged.” Nearby cases can differ slightly even when the difference does not change a yes-or-no answer.

A complete treatment must also explain how claims combine and what an inference preserves. Drawing a smooth curve does not settle those questions. Established degree-theoretic approaches provide formal possibilities, while other accounts explain vagueness without requiring a continuum of truth values.[5]

For the cognitive account, the useful question is how a system preserves relevant gradation while still producing categorical answers when a task calls for them. A practical decision may require “pour” or “stop” even though the represented amount varies continuously.

Neither a categorical action nor an unchanged verbal answer tells us, by itself, that the underlying representation is all-or-nothing.

## Using an assessment to choose the next question

An assessment can guide inquiry by indicating what is missing.

Not knowing how much water is in a glass calls for a better look or a measurement. Not knowing what someone means by “full” calls for clarification. Agreeing about the amount but differing over how much a recipe needs calls for examining the requirements of the task.

Disagreement between people can expose similar distinctions. Two people debating whether a system is reliable enough to use may mean different properties. One means that it stays online; the other means that its answers are correct. They might instead have different test results, or agree about its errors while disagreeing about acceptable risks.

Those differences do not all call for more of the same evidence. Nor are they necessarily failures of reasoning. People can make reasonable judgments from different information, and a genuine conflict of priorities can remain after the facts are shared.

The proposed practical use of CST is to help identify which question needs attention next. Whether it improves that choice beyond existing communication and metacognitive approaches is something to test.

## What would make the account useful?

A model must specify what its representation contains and what difference that content makes.

Two equally confident assessments might preserve different qualifications and therefore direct different checks. A known intermediate value should be distinguishable from uncertainty about a binary condition. A diagnostic method should choose a useful intervention before seeing whether it works, rather than explaining the outcome afterward.

Existing memory and metacognitive accounts may already explain some or all of those observations. Some of the project’s current mathematical models make the same predictions as ordinary selective-access accounts. Calling one a correspondence schema does not distinguish them.

The aim is therefore an explicit, testable connection among meaning, assessment, and inquiry. Its foundations are inherited; its proposed additions need their own justification.

## When the feeling comes apart from its grounds

An account that starts with useful correspondence must also explain departures from it. People make mistakes, misremember details, and sometimes have compelling experiences that do not match what is happening around them. Hallucination is an important example of why the force of an experience cannot itself certify its object. These are different problems, not interchangeable names for one failure.

A judgment can also survive after its qualifications have been forgotten. Someone may retain “that source was convincing” without retaining what the source actually established. The assessment remains usable, but its apparent reach can exceed its grounds.

These possibilities concern the relationship between an assessment and what warrants it. They are different from the glass being genuinely partly filled.

### Truth feelings are also shaped by social life

We evaluate claims while learning from others, defending positions, making commitments, and deciding whom to trust. Social life can improve correspondence: testimony, criticism, and shared investigation give us access to information we would not acquire alone. It can also influence an assessment in ways that serve something other than accuracy.

In one study, an opportunity to persuade others increased privately elicited overconfidence about participants’ performance. The influence was not limited to stronger public wording.[6] Other research found similar self-persuasion when people summarized a position rather than tried to persuade someone of it, making exposure and processing important alternative explanations.[7]

Commitment introduces another possibility. Being regarded as dependable can be socially valuable, and evolutionary models examine conditions under which apparently unconditional cooperation provides advantages.[8] But commitment to an action need not require certainty about every belief associated with it. Nor does reputation always favor refusing to investigate: people can gain reputational benefits by considering opposing evidence before judging a wrongdoer.[9]

The biological and social explanations may be closely related. A capacity shaped by evolution could be responsive to learned expectations about trust, loyalty, fairness, or accuracy. Cultural practices could then help determine when firmness is rewarded and when continued investigation is expected. This is a possible relationship among levels of explanation—not evidence that a particular history or shared mechanism has already been established.

For CST, the important question is how these influences affect the assessment: its apparent certainty, the qualifications retained, its public expression, or the decision to keep checking. Those effects need not move together.

None of this implies that a socially shaped judgment is necessarily false. It means that understanding why a judgment feels true requires examining more than the claim and the evidence immediately in front of the evaluator.

## An account that applies to itself

CST begins with the possibility of successful correspondence and asks how a physical mind represents and uses an assessment of it. Graded satisfaction, uncertainty, and categorical action occupy different roles within that account. Errors and social or biological influences raise further questions about when the assessment remains well grounded and when it does not.

The theory receives no exemption from those questions. Finding it convincing is another assessment to examine.

Its current contribution is a proposed integration, not a uniquely identified brain mechanism or a demonstration that all ordinary truth is graded. Progress depends on making its claims precise enough to distinguish them from alternatives—and on being willing to revise the account when those comparisons go against it.
