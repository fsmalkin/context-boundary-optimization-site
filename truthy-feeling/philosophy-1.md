## The question

Suppose you know how much water a glass contains and describe it accurately. Start with that success: a claim gets something about its subject right, and a person can recognize and use the relationship. What does correctness consist in, and how could a physical evaluator represent it? We will develop that account before considering the substantial ways conviction can diverge from it.

The philosophical side of CST starts by distinguishing the claim, the subject matter it concerns, and the act of evaluating it. Its positive proposal is that these relationships can belong within a physical account, and that some relations of correctness may admit degrees. The cognitive side asks how a mind could represent and use them. This post develops the philosophical argument without assuming that a model of the mind has already proved it. Starting with an accurate case is not a claim that human judgments are uniformly reliable.

The map–territory distinction separates a representation from what it represents; it does not put the evaluator outside the world. Yudkowsky's account already treats beliefs as physical and describes evaluation through models of beliefs and reality.[13] CST takes that as a starting point rather than a metaphor awaiting its first scientific explanation. The further questions concern the kind of satisfaction a claim admits, the conditions fixing its meaning, and which information about an assessment guides inquiry. Felt fit and the correctness of the claim remain distinct even when the evaluator gets it right.

## Words, claims, and what they concern

A sentence is an expression; a claim is what we use that expression to say. “It is full” leaves much to its setting. We need to know what “it” refers to and whether “full” means filled to capacity, sufficiently filled for a purpose, or filled to some degree. Clarification may recover an intended question or lead the speakers to adopt a new one; it is not automatically new evidence about an unchanged proposition.

Interpretation and relevant conditions fix what a claim concerns. For a local weather prediction, place and time matter. The forecast supplies information about that weather; treating it as the standard of correctness would let a mistaken forecast make itself correct. We may call the selected subject matter a reference domain, but no separate ontological realm is needed to explain these conditions.

Fiction complicates the relationship. A story is not merely an imperfect report about a hidden historical person. It helps constitute the fictional setting we discuss. Conan Doyle's descriptions warrant calling Holmes a detective, but do not place a physical Holmes in the world.[1] Texts and readers exist; the represented person need not have lived. A claim such as “Holmes is dead” may require a particular source, narrative point, and a distinction between what happened and what a narrator believes. Making those conditions explicit does not itself establish the claim.

This avoids multiplying worlds without making interpretation arbitrary. We still need to explain why a reading is warranted, how versions are distinguished, and which questions remain unsettled. “According to the story” helps specify what is asserted; it is not a complete theory of fictional truth. Nor does the fact that a finite sentence omits most details make it indeterminate: finite expressions can make determinate claims about infinite structures. Implicit interpretation and incomplete description are different matters.

The proposal is correspondence in this broad sense of getting the relevant subject matter right. It is not visual resemblance or word overlap. A statement and its negation can share nearly all their words while making incompatible claims. What matters is the interpreted relation, not the similarity of the marks.

## The partiality can belong to the condition

Suppose a glass holds 250 milliliters and contains exactly 200. Its fill fraction is 0.8. There is no uncertainty in this example. We can use that fraction as an explicitly stipulated degree of fullness, while also saying that the crisp condition “filled to the brim” is not satisfied. Accurately judging an intermediate degree is a case of successful evaluation, not an error caused by the value being intermediate.

Fuzzy membership supplies a formal way to represent such degrees.[2] A membership function assigns an object a degree of applicability of a predicate. That is different from a probability distribution over possible states. A precise degree of 0.8 does not mean that an unseen all-or-nothing condition holds in eight of ten possible cases.

The glass's physical scale does not dictate the interpretation of every sentence containing “full.” A comparison standard may depend on context or an endpoint. Linguistic work on gradable predicates separates these features.[3] Someone can accept that filling has degrees yet hold that an ordinary assertion of “full” is true or false according to a standard. That is a substantive alternative, not a misunderstanding we can dismiss.

CST's preferred hypothesis goes further: some interpreted claims have graded satisfaction as their appropriate semantic value. “Semantic” here concerns what the claim means and how its correctness is determined. Cintula and colleagues develop this position in relation to gradable language, beyond a simple membership illustration.[10] Perfect information may establish an intermediate value rather than force a binary verdict. The argument needs a justified interpretation, not a curve fitted to responses and then declared correct because it fits them.

Smith provides a bridge between degree and uncertainty: degree of belief can be the expected semantic value across the possibilities an agent considers.[11] Being certain of an intermediate value and being uncertain between true and false can then produce the same average without being the same state of knowledge. A single rating need not tell us which explanation applies. This is a developed philosophical proposal, not evidence that every person's confidence is computed in that way.

This also distinguishes membership from typicality. A penguin can be an unusual bird without being only partly a bird. Research on concepts motivates considering graded structure, but it does not make every comparison of resemblance a truth degree.[4] Partial neural activation is a possible implementation of a graded assessment, not a philosophical proof that every truth is continuous.

## What happens to the paradox of the heap?

Consider a glass that is clearly nearly full. Removing one drop seems insufficient to change that description. If every such step leaves the description unchanged, repeated removal appears to preserve “nearly full” until the glass is empty. This is the structure of a Sorites argument.

A degree account distinguishes closeness from exact preservation. Two adjacent cases can be almost alike while lying at different points on a scale. A person may not notice the difference or change a categorical answer. Neither fact establishes that applicability is identical in both cases.

But introducing degrees is not yet a complete solution. A formal account must say what “if” means in the tolerance premise and which values an inference is required to preserve. The next philosophy post uses an established conditional that treats unchanged applicability as fully preserved and a small decrease as nearly preserved. It distinguishes ways of combining those steps instead of assuming that every logical operation must accumulate small losses in the same way.[10]

There are established rivals. An epistemic approach can hold that a boundary is sharp but unknowable. Other logical approaches revise how premises and conclusions are treated without requiring a continuum of truth values.[5] Sorites therefore gives us a problem to explain, not a demonstration that CST alone is possible. The project connects an account of gradation with an account of the evaluator's representation, local tolerance, and categorical response; it does not claim to invent degree-theoretic solutions.

## Does truth depend on agreement?

We can now state the ideal-evaluation principle. Fix the interpreted claim and relevant evaluation conditions. Supply accurate information sufficient for the evaluation, and require the specified reasoning to finish without error. Under appropriate formal conditions, each adequate evaluator recovers the same value. That may be true, false, or a determinate intermediate degree.

The agreement follows from evaluating the same relationship adequately. It does not create the relationship. An actual community can agree because its inquiry is good, or because its members share a mistake. Nor does persistent disagreement establish that there is no fact to discover: people may have different information, interpretations, or methods.

Peirce's limit-of-inquiry account is an important predecessor. He tied truth to what inquiry would ultimately settle upon, rather than to the current beliefs of a particular community.[6] Our principle is a more limited conditional statement. It does not prove that actual inquiry will reach those conditions, or that every meaningful question has an answer available to a finite procedure.

“Perfect information” need not mean knowing the entire universe. It means what the specified evaluation requires. And these are sufficient conditions for a guarantee, not necessary conditions for every correct belief. We can know a particular answer without possessing a method guaranteed to answer everything. Tarski likewise distinguished an account of truth from a universal means of recognizing it.[7]

Objectivity, determinacy, and accessibility consequently come apart. A claim can have a determinate value that does not depend on my approval, while I lack the evidence or method needed to find it. Under a graded semantics that determinate value need not be an endpoint.

## What kind of disagreement remains?

These distinctions could help identify what a dispute needs next. Consider “this system is reliable enough to deploy.” One person may mean uptime and another answer accuracy. Once they clarify that, they may still disagree about the test results, how testing predicts real use, or what error rate is acceptable. Clarification, evidence exchange, model comparison, and discussion of trade-offs address different parts of the dispute.

A difference in meaning is not always a trivial misunderstanding. Plunkett and Sundell argue that people can substantively disagree over which concepts should govern a conversation.[9] Two speakers may understand each other's standards and still dispute which is appropriate. A different standard for a word changes the interpreted claim; a different policy for acting on an agreed estimate need not change its semantic value.

CST's proposed diagnostic use is therefore provisional. Several differences may coexist, and sometimes there is insufficient information to locate them. People can agree about the facts while retaining a genuine disagreement about values. The goal is a better account of what remains contested, not to label every opponent mistaken or to manufacture agreement. Whether the proposed representation improves that diagnosis is an empirical question, not a result of the ideal-evaluation argument.

## What the cognitive account contributes

A philosophical account can distinguish these possibilities, but cannot determine by definition which distinctions a brain represents. The cognitive companion proposes a simplified model of the evaluation itself. It asks whether a system preserves a degree, uncertainty about it, a source qualification, or merely a verdict, and how that information influences inquiry.

The general connection has close predecessors. Mangan's work already links felt rightness, contextual fit and the control of ongoing thought.[12] CST builds on that tradition rather than discovering it. Its proposed contribution is a more explicit connection between semantic satisfaction, represented conditions and the selection of inquiry. Whether the resulting account adds to existing mechanisms remains a question to test.

This is where the two papers genuinely need one another. A numerical response may be an estimate of partial applicability, a probability about a binary fact, or a reporting convention. The semantic distinction tells the experiment what it must discriminate. An empirical finding can then change the proposed mechanism or show that an existing memory or metacognitive account is sufficient. A useful method for classifying disagreements could likewise improve discussion without proving that the brain implements that classification as a schema.

## When correctness and conviction diverge

The successful case does not make an evaluator infallible. The account must also allow a claim to fit an inaccurate internal representation, a source to be misleading, or a relevant condition to be misunderstood or lost. These are departures from tracking the appropriate subject matter, not consequences of a semantic value falling between zero and one. The correctness criterion must remain independent of how convincing the result feels.

Social purposes raise a further question. An incentive to persuade could change how an assessment is expressed, how certain it feels, or which evidence is sought. None of those effects changes the semantic value merely because conviction is useful. Nor must a social influence impair evaluation; discussion and checking may improve it. The cognitive companion examines these possibilities and the evidence distinguishing them.

On the physicalist account, those social processes operate through biological systems. Explaining their implementation, their development through learning and their possible evolutionary origins are related tasks, not rival labels for the same result. A proposed historical explanation requires more than showing a present-day benefit. This extension belongs alongside the account of error and conviction, not as a consequence of gradation or a premise of the ideal-evaluation argument.

## A position that includes its own uncertainty

Physicalism is a substantive starting position here: understanding, representation, evaluation, and their experienced character belong within the physical world. It does not follow that a successful response model proves the whole position, or that naming a representation explains its content. Those remain obligations of the paired account.

CST must also apply to its own acceptance. The fact that it seems coherent gives us something to investigate, not a certificate of correctness. This is fallibilism: holding conclusions on the strength of their grounds while allowing them to be revised. Peirce applied that attitude to his own claims about cognition.[8]

The philosophical gain is a connected picture rather than a universal answer machine. A claim concerns something; its correctness is constrained by that subject under an interpretation; a physical evaluator approaches the relationship through evidence and inference. Some of those relationships may be graded, and some guarantees may be unavailable. Neither possibility requires us to identify truth with confidence or to give up on better-grounded judgment.
